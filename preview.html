Jawahar Singh, Ph.D.

Engineering how plants make their own fertilizer.

Legumes have evolved one of nature's most remarkable partnerships — working with soil bacteria to turn atmospheric nitrogen into a form plants can use. My research asks a bigger question: can we understand this system well enough to engineer it?

I study how nutrient signals, developmental programmes and plant–microbe interactions control nitrogen-fixing root nodules — combining functional genomics, cell biology and genome engineering to find the molecular constraints on symbiosis, and how they might be rewired in crop legumes.

The challenge

Agriculture needs nitrogen. Plants already know how to make it available.

Modern agriculture depends heavily on synthetic nitrogen fertilizer — energy-intensive to produce and a major source of emissions and pollution. Legumes offer a fundamentally different solution. Through root nodules, they establish a partnership with rhizobia that enables biological nitrogen fixation.

But this system is not simply switched on. The plant continuously integrates information about its nutritional status, developmental state and environment before deciding whether to build and maintain a nodule. My research focuses on understanding that decision — because if we can identify the molecular constraints controlling nitrogen fixation, we may be able to engineer legumes that acquire more nitrogen while needing less fertilizer.

The question

When does a plant decide to invest in nitrogen fixation?

A root nodule is more than a structure that houses bacteria. It is a tightly regulated organ whose development forces the plant to coordinate many processes at once:

  • Nutrient availability
  • Hormonal signalling
  • Immune regulation
  • Cell differentiation
  • Microbial recognition
  • Nutrient transport

My work sits at the intersection of these processes — and centres on one question: how does a plant balance the cost of building and maintaining a nitrogen-fixing nodule against the nutritional benefit it provides? Understanding that balance could reveal new ways to improve symbiotic performance.

Research discoveries

Three discoveries shaping my programme

01

Phosphate can gate whether nodulation happens

Plants do not treat nitrogen fixation independently of their own nutritional status. In common bean, I found that the phosphate starvation–response transcription factor PvPHR-L7 binds the P1BS cis-element in the promoters of the nodulation master-regulator NIN and the autoregulation gene TML. Knocking it down increases nodulation; over-expressing it decreases nodulation — placing phosphate signalling directly upstream of the decision to nodulate.

The broader idea: phosphate availability can act as a gatekeeper for symbiotic investment — a previously underappreciated link between plant nutrient homeostasis and nitrogen-fixing symbiosis.

Read the paper → Singh et al., Plant Cell Physiol. 2025
02

Nodulation begins with molecular recognition

During my PhD I investigated how chickpea perceives rhizobial Nod factors. I identified and characterised a family of 17 LysM receptor-like kinases and studied their roles in the signalling that connects microbial recognition at the root surface to downstream developmental responses — showing that knockdown of CaNFP and CaLYK3 reduces nodulation. This work helped define the receptor toolkit for Nod-factor perception in a non-model crop legume.

Read the paper → Singh & Verma, Environ. Exp. Bot. 2022
03

The next step is engineering the system

At the Sainsbury Laboratory I am taking these questions from discovery toward engineering. My current work studies conserved regulatory mechanisms that constrain nitrogen fixation in crop legumes — cowpea and soybean — asking which genes limit nodule performance, whether those constraints can be removed, and whether regulatory pathways can be tuned rather than simply switched on or off.

Current research

From understanding nodulation to engineering it

The transition I see as central to the future of symbiosis research: from describing regulatory networks to experimentally rewiring them.

Cowpea

A major food and forage legume of importance for smallholder agriculture, particularly across Africa.

Soybean

One of the world's most important protein and oil crops, with large potential for improving biological nitrogen fixation.

Functional genomics

Identifying the genes and regulatory elements that control nodule formation, maintenance and nitrogen fixation.

Genome engineering

Using CRISPR-Cas to test whether manipulating these regulatory mechanisms can improve symbiotic performance.

The goal is not simply to make plants with more nodules — it is to understand what makes a nodule productive, persistent and nutritionally valuable.

The research programme I want to build

Engineering nutrient-smart symbiosis

My long-term goal is an independent programme at the interface of plant nutrient signalling, cell biology, symbiosis and genome engineering. The central idea is simple: a nodule is an engineered biological organ whose performance is controlled by the plant's nutritional and developmental state. I want to understand that control system — and determine whether it can be redesigned.

01

Discover — what limits nodule performance?

Identify the genetic and cellular mechanisms that control nodule formation, differentiation, longevity and nitrogen fixation.

Nutrient signalling Cell-type regulation Nodule longevity
02

Decode — how do nutrient signals converge on symbiosis?

Plants sense nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, iron and more at once. I want to understand how those signals are integrated at the cellular level to decide whether nodules are initiated, maintained or restricted.

Transcriptomics Single-cell & spatial Genetic perturbation
03

Engineer — can we rewire the regulatory system?

Once the limiting mechanisms are known, the question is whether they can be modified — not to make biology behave unnaturally, but to understand its control points well enough to make useful changes.

CRISPR-Cas editing Promoter engineering Synthetic elements
04

Translate — can better symbiosis cut fertilizer dependence?

Engineered traits must work beyond controlled conditions. The programme connects molecular discovery to crop performance under nutrient limitation and stress — from molecular mechanism to agricultural trait.

Nutrient limitation Field relevance Food security

About

From studying stress biology to engineering symbiosis

My scientific journey has taken me across three countries and through several levels of plant biology. I began with stress physiology and proteomics in India. During my PhD at the National Institute of Plant Genome Research (New Delhi), I moved into legume biology and the molecular mechanisms of Nod-factor perception in chickpea. In Mexico, I studied how phosphate availability influences nodulation in common bean — work that led to a direct connection between phosphate starvation signalling and the master regulator of nodulation. At Cambridge, I am now translating these regulatory principles into genetic strategies for improving nitrogen fixation in crop legumes.

Each stage has changed the question I ask: from how plants respond to stress, to how they establish symbiosis, to how we might engineer that symbiosis for agriculture.
